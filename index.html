Who arranges the ride?

How Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Connecticut arrange non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) for Medicaid members: who runs it, who holds the contracts, and what is changing in 2025–26. Current as of October 21, 2026.

Delivery models: four of the five states hand rides to a broker. New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut each use one broker statewide, and Massachusetts uses two regional transit authorities. Pennsylvania runs rides through its 67 counties, with a state broker only in Philadelphia, and a 2026 state study recommended keeping that model.

Contracts and procurement: New York's broker contracts end in July 2027, and the base term in Connecticut's 2023 RFP ends that September. Procurement has been contested in three states: New York's 2022 award drew a protest; Pennsylvania cancelled two Philadelphia solicitations after protests, and a third is stayed; and in New Jersey, an August 2026 protest decision sent the rebid back for award to the incumbent.

Three terms, briefly

Non-emergency medical transportation

Medicaid must ensure members can get to covered medical care. NEMT covers rides to appointments, from public transit passes and mileage to wheelchair vans and stretcher transport, for members with no other way to get there.

Brokers

Companies or public agencies that take ride requests, check eligibility, assign trips to local transportation providers, and report on performance. States pay them a fixed amount per member, at cost, or through a mix.

Carve-outs

When a state removes a benefit from its managed care contracts and delivers it separately. Several states carve NEMT out of managed care so one broker or program handles rides for most members.

2025–26 activity
Legislation and studies Act 46 of 2025 required a study of a statewide broker. The June 2026 study found the risks significantly outweighed the benefits and described ongoing work to improve the county model instead: updated MATP standards, PennDOT review of policy changes, and a Find My Ride module for mileage reimbursement requests. It put SFY 2025 spending at about $133.5 million for 4.1 million trips, about $32.65 per trip. S4862 and A7329 would remove the requirement in Social Services Law § 365-h that brokers manage Medicaid transportation; both were re-referred to the Health committees on Jan. 7, 2026. A May 2025 policy limits covered rides to opioid treatment and outpatient programs to the member's local medical market area. A985 (introduced Jan. 13, 2026) would set broker vehicle, staffing and performance standards and require reports to the Department of Human Services; a similar 2024 bill, A5032, did not advance past committee. No NEMT-specific legislation or budget action found. From Jan. 1, 2026, members who lost Senior Care Options eligibility book rides through the HST brokers. HB 5356 (2026) would require the Department of Social Services to review NEMT rates at least annually and would let drivers with a pending taxi or livery license endorsement provide NEMT for up to 90 days; it cleared Human Services 21–2 and was tabled for the House calendar on April 7, 2026. A 2025 bill with a similar driver provision, SB 1298, cleared committee 22–0; final action not found.

What stands out

Brokers are the norm; Pennsylvania is the exception

Four states route rides through a broker. Pennsylvania's legislature stopped a statewide broker in 2019, and the state's June 2026 study found a broker's risks outweighed its benefits, including an estimated gain in federal matching funds, and focused on improving the county system.

Managed care hands off the ride

New York carved rides out of managed long-term care in 2024, New Jersey's rules carve them out of managed care, and Pennsylvania's counties serve plan members. Massachusetts is the partial exception: its integrated plans for older adults and people with disabilities arrange their own members' rides.

Procurement is the pressure point

Broker contracts are large, multi-year, and contested. New York's award was protested, Pennsylvania's Philadelphia solicitation has failed twice and is now in litigation, and New Jersey's rebid turned on a protest over a missing subcontractor form, while the contract that began in 2017 has run on change orders.

Payment models differ

Connecticut's RFP weighed a per-member-per-month model with a separate administrative budget against full capitation, with surpluses above 5% returned to the state; Philadelphia's broker is paid a capitated per-member-per-month rate; and Massachusetts pays its brokers at cost for transportation, plus a management fee and a shared-ride incentive. The model shapes who carries the risk when ride volume rises.

Legislatures are writing accountability rules

New Jersey's A985 would set broker performance standards, Connecticut's HB 5356 would require annual rate reviews, and New York's S4862 and A7329 would remove the broker requirement altogether. Massachusetts' auditor found overpayments, and Connecticut's oversight council reviews performance in public.

What to watch

  • Pending: New Jersey's final award on remand under bid 24DPP01040, which had not been posted as of Oct. 20, 2026. The current contract extension runs through Feb. 27, 2027.
  • Pending: the litigation over Pennsylvania's Philadelphia solicitation (RFA 06-24), and DHS follow-through on updated MATP standards and the Find My Ride mileage reimbursement module.
  • 2027 sessions: New Jersey's A985, New York's S4862 and A7329, and whether Connecticut's rate-review proposal returns.
  • July 31, 2027: New York's two broker contracts end; a rebid has not been posted.
  • Sept. 30, 2027: the end of the base term set in Connecticut's 2023 RFP, with two one-year extensions available.

The question I expect to shape the next cycle: whether states keep buying medical transportation as a standalone contract, or start tying ride performance to the managed care and long-term care decisions that depend on it.

How this was compiled

Scope. Five states, three areas: how Medicaid NEMT is delivered, the broker contracts and procurements, and 2025–26 legislation, studies, and oversight. Emergency ambulance and non-medical waiver transportation are noted only where they mark the edge of the NEMT program.

Sources. Facts are drawn from state Medicaid agency pages, procurement notices, contract records, statutes and regulations, legislative records, audits, and federal filings. Where a detail rests on a secondary source, the table says "as reported." Where a detail could not be confirmed, such as an unpublished contract term, the page says so rather than guessing.

Neutrality. Brokers, transportation providers, health plans, hospitals, counties, and members all have stakes in how these programs are run. This page describes how states have chosen to deliver rides, not which model or vendor is better.

The underlying data is in medicaid-transportation.csv. This comparison is for policy analysis and is not legal or regulatory advice.
